## 0. The rules that matter most for editwright

1. Work big to small. Structure first, then lines, then copy, then proof. CIEP: plan and develop, then fix the detail, then finalise and publish. Polishing sentences before the structure settles wastes the work. ([CIEP, Proofreading or Editing?, 2020 rev. 2021, p. 4](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf))
2. Do not change voice. CIEP: "Experienced editors should not change your tone of voice or writing style (unless it's not right for your intended readership)." (same PDF, p. 6)
3. Query when unsure; do not weaponise queries. ACES lists seven rules: be valid, clear, concise, humble, efficient, polite, selective. A query is not valid if it forces the editor's preference or silences the author's voice. ([ACES, 2021](https://aceseditors.org/news/2021/author-queries-for-the-nonfiction-editor-how-to-raise-em-and-how-to-phrase-em))
4. Readers advise and authors decide. SFWA on sensitivity reading: readers "offer advice", and "it's up to the authors to make any suggested changes." ([SFWA, 2024-04-02](https://sfwa.org/2024/04/02/what-is-sensitivity-reading/))
5. Stay inside the level that was asked for. CIEP's copyediting list puts "substantial rewriting" and "development of or planning the content" out of scope. Its proofreading list puts "rephrasing to flow or sound better" out of scope. (CIEP PDF, pp. 16 and 17)
6. There are few rights and wrongs. CIEP: "there are very few 'rights' and 'wrongs' in good editing. Consistency, clarity and context are key." A good editor knows a rule from a preference. (CIEP PDF, p. 5)

## 1. Levels of edit and where each stops

### 1.1 What the bodies say

**EFA (Editorial Freelancers Association, US).** Source: [EFA editorial services definitions](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/) and [EFA rates chart](https://www.the-efa.org/rates/).

- Developmental editing (also "substantive", "structural", "content") deals with "content, organization, and genre considerations". The deliverable is a "manuscript evaluation" (a critique) or a "revision (or 'editorial' or 'edit') letter" with suggestions.
- Line editing works "at the sentence or paragraph level". The main focus is "improving the language and style of the text". It can be sold alone, after a developmental edit, or with a copyedit.
- Copyediting covers "correcting spelling, grammar, usage, and punctuation, checking cross-references", and preparing style sheets.
- Proofreading checks "for typographical errors and formatting mistakes" near publication. It compares stages to confirm that changes were made.
- Fact-checking confirms "the veracity of each statement that is claimed as an assertion of fact". This is a separate service, deeper than the incidental checks an editor makes.
- Authenticity reading looks for portrayals that "might misrepresent people from a given group". Members of that community usually do it.
- Sensitivity reading looks for portrayals that "might offend readers". The EFA says people outside the author's community generally do it.
- Beta reading flags "errors, plot problems, inconsistencies, or unclear sections".
- Category split on the rates chart: manuscript assessment/evaluation sits under **Reading**, not Editing. Developmental, line, copy and proof sit under **Editing**. This is the EFA's own line between critique and edit.

**CIEP (Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading, UK; known as SfEP until the 2019 Royal Charter).** Source: [CIEP, Proofreading or Editing?](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf), pp. 2-6 and 16-17.

- Three stages: **Plan and develop** ("The big picture. You're not fixing detail at this stage"). Roles in this stage: development editor, structural editor, substantive editor, content editor, manuscript critique, review or appraisal, beta or test reader, authenticity reader.
- **Fix the detail.** Copyeditor, *line editor*, proof-editor. "There may be a lot of changes at this stage, so it needs to happen *before* final design or layout."
- **Finalise and publish.** The proofreader works "*after* layout/formatting".
- Copyediting "is professional help to make a text ready for publication by ensuring that it's clear, consistent, correct and complete" (p. 2).
- Copyediting **is**: working on raw or draft text; changes "for flow, tone and intended readership"; applying house style; marking up structure; listing permissions needed; formatting references; "querying obvious factual inaccuracies"; flagging legal issues (plagiarism, copyright, libel).
- Copyediting **is not**: working on final layout; "substantial rewriting"; developing or planning content; writing a comprehensive style guide for the client; getting permissions; checking that references are accurate; "doing substantial research or fact-checking"; solving legal issues.
- Proofreading **is**: working on edited text in final layout; essential spelling, grammar and punctuation errors; "minor changes for sense"; checking that page elements are consistent and that style decisions were applied.
- Proofreading **is not**: working on raw text; "changing your mind on spelling preferences"; "rephrasing to flow or sound better"; reordering structure; adding text; checking that references are accurate.
- CIEP FAQ wording: proofreading is "the final stage in the process of producing a piece of text ... before it's published". ([CIEP FAQs](https://www.ciep.uk/about/faqs)) CIEP also says "proofreading is like wrestling in a broom cupboard". ([CIEP FAQ](https://www.ciep.uk/resource/what-is-the-difference-between-copyediting-and-proofreading.html))

**Chicago Manual of Style, 18th edition (September 2024).** Source: [CMOS What's New](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/help-tools/what-s-new.html). The full text is paywalled.

- Chapter 2 is "Manuscript Preparation, Manuscript Editing, and Proofreading". New in the 18th edition: authenticity and sensitivity readings (2.54), accessibility checks (2.143), audiobook preparation (2.144), and self-publishing steps.
- Chicago no longer recommends generic angle-bracket labels as copyediting markup. They are still fine for figure callouts.
- Fiction coverage is expanded: interrupted speech and stuttering, speaker tags, multi-paragraph dialogue, text messages, and italics for internal thought.
- AI: authors must "specify whether and to what extent AI has been used" (4.51). There is a new citation section for AI-generated content (14.112).
- Secondary report: CMOS 2.53 splits copyediting into *mechanical* editing (grammar, spelling and the like, plus applying a style) and *substantive* editing (presentation, restructuring sentences, word choice). Unverified against the paid text; the claim comes via [Wikipedia, Copy editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy_editing).
- 2025/2026: no 19th edition has been announced, and the What's New page lists no dated online revisions after 2024. CMOS Shop Talk runs a "Fiction+" series for fiction questions. ([Shop Talk, Saller, 2022-12-13](https://cmosshoptalk.com/2022/12/13/advice-for-fiction-writers-and-editors-chicago-style/))

**ACES: The Society for Editing (US).** I found no formal taxonomy of levels on aceseditors.org. Its "About" page and its guide for new editors returned 404 or held no definitions. ACES is useful for querying craft (see section 2). Treat ACES as silent on level boundaries.

### 1.2 Where the bodies disagree

- **Line editing.** The EFA treats it as its own service, focused on "language and style". CIEP places the line editor inside "fix the detail", next to the copyeditor. So in UK usage a copyedit often includes flow and tone, and CIEP's copyediting list includes "making changes for flow, tone and intended readership". An editwright "line" pass and "copy" pass may overlap; say which is meant.
- **Critique versus developmental edit.** The EFA files critique under *Reading*. CIEP lists "manuscript critique" in the same *plan and develop* stage as development editing. Both agree a critique does not mark up text.
- **Sensitivity versus authenticity.** The EFA separates them by who does the reading (outsider versus community member). SFWA uses "sensitivity reading" for the lived-experience consultant role. Chicago 2.54 names both, but its text is unverified.
- **Fact-checking.** CIEP's copyeditor queries "obvious factual inaccuracies" and does no "substantial research or fact-checking". The EFA lists fact-checking as a separate research service.

### 1.3 Scope table for editwright

| Level | Touches | Deliverable | Must not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manuscript assessment (critique) | Whole work, read once or twice | Report, "usually no more than 10 pages" ([Jane Friedman, Chauncey, upd. 2025-02-18](https://janefriedman.com/manuscript-evaluations-what-they-are-and-what-to-expect/)); light illustrative markup at most | Line edits |
| Developmental (structural) | Concept, structure, plot, character, POV, pacing, genre fit | Editorial letter plus margin comments; letters can exceed 30 pages (same Friedman source) | Fix commas; rewrite prose |
| Line | Sentences and paragraphs: rhythm, clarity, word choice, repetition, voice consistency | Tracked suggestions plus comments | Restructure the plot; impose the editor's style |
| Copy | Correctness and consistency, house or author style, cross-references, continuity | Tracked changes, queries, **style sheet** | Substantial rewriting; research |
| Proofread | Laid-out pages: typos, layout, applied style | Marked proof | Rephrase; change style decisions |
| Fact-check | Each assertion of fact | Source log per claim | Edit prose |
| Sensitivity / authenticity read | Portrayal of a group | Report plus comments, including what works | Decide or rewrite |

Pace and price give a sense of how deep each level goes. These are EFA fiction medians from a survey run November 2025 to mid-January 2026, on 250-word pages. Developmental: 5.5 to 10 pages an hour, 3.0 to 3.5 cents a word. Line: 5.0 to 9.5 pages an hour. Copy: 6 to 10 pages an hour. Proof: 10 to 15 pages an hour. Assessment: 1.5 to 1.8 cents a word. Beta read: 16 to 40 pages an hour. ([EFA rates](https://www.the-efa.org/rates/)) A fetch tool extracted these figures; spot-check them before quoting them to users.

## 2. How a professional delivers each level

### 2.1 Editorial letter (developmental) and assessment report

- An assessment letter "typically runs a few pages, usually no more than 10 pages". A developmental edit involves "multiple deep reads", hundreds of comments, and sometimes a letter of more than 30 pages. ([Jane Friedman site, Sarah Chauncey, 2019, upd. 2025-02-18](https://janefriedman.com/manuscript-evaluations-what-they-are-and-what-to-expect/))
- Other norms reported: 5 to 7 pages, or 5 to 10 pages, with in-manuscript notes. These come from vendor pages and are unverified. ([Editorworld](https://www.editorworld.com/article/manuscript-critique-vs-editorial-letter-vs-developmental-edit))
- Usual structure, synthesised from the sources above and from [Scott Norton, *Developmental Editing*, 2nd ed., U. Chicago Press, 2023](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo201563215.html), whose second edition adds a fiction chapter:
  1. What the book is: a one-paragraph read-back of premise, genre and intended reader. This proves the editor understood the book.
  2. Strengths, specific and with page references.
  3. Big issues in priority order, usually three to five. For each: the symptom with examples, why it costs the reader, and two or more options. The author chooses.
  4. Element-by-element notes: structure, character, POV, pacing, setting, dialogue, voice, genre conventions.
  5. Revision plan: an order of operations.
  6. Answers to any questions the author asked.
- Margin comments point to the letter ("see letter, section 2"). They do not repeat it.

### 2.2 Margin comments and author queries

- "AU:" addresses the author ("AU: Revision OK?"). This is a long-standing convention in publishing, not a Chicago rule. ([Cell Press Crosstalk](https://crosstalk.cell.com/blog/an-authors-guide-to-copyediting-queries))
- ACES's three essentials are tone, specificity and tact. ([ACES, McCowan, 2019](https://aceseditors.org/news/2019/the-3-essential-elements-of-author-queries))
  - Bad: "Revise." Good: name what jumps and propose an order.
  - Bad: "Huh?" Good: "This section is a bit vaguely worded, whereas preceding paragraphs are very clear. Here's a suggestion..."
- ACES's seven rules (Sue's Golden Rules) give a template that offers options: "I'm not sure what you mean here. Might it be [X] or perhaps [Y] or something else? Please advise." ([ACES, 2021](https://aceseditors.org/news/2021/author-queries-for-the-nonfiction-editor-how-to-raise-em-and-how-to-phrase-em)) The rules also say to number queries serially, group them by chapter, log their dates and the answers, and accept the author's rejections gracefully.
- CIEP: editors are trained "to deal with author queries and amendments sensitively". (CIEP PDF, p. 5) CIEP sells a members' fact sheet, "Good practice for author queries". It is paywalled and unread. ([CIEP](https://ciep.uk/product/good-practice-for-author-queries.html))
- When to query and when to change: change silently only what is objectively wrong *and* covered by the agreed style, such as typos, house-style spellings and punctuation conventions. Query anything that touches meaning, voice, fact, or a deliberate-looking choice. This is synthesised from ACES rule 1 and CIEP's "rule versus preference" line, and is not a quoted rule. For fiction, practitioners query more often and offer rephrasings rather than rewrites. ([Louise Harnby, querying for fiction copyeditors](https://louiseharnbyproofreader.com/blog/how-to-query-like-a-superhero-5-tips-for-new-fiction-copyeditors-and-proofreaders): the page returned 403, so this rests on the search snippet and is unverified)

### 2.3 Style sheet (copyedit) and story bible

- The EFA names style sheets as a copyediting deliverable. ([EFA definitions](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/)) The EFA also runs a webinar, "Copyediting Fiction Part 2: The Fiction Style Sheet". ([EFA](https://the-efa.org/product/copyediting-fiction-part-2-the-fiction-style-sheet-webinar-sp22))
- A Penguin Random House copy editor keeps a running document of "chronology, logistics, plot, and personal details". They deliver a style sheet ("every proper noun in the book" plus odd words) and a **separate timeline**. Example catches: breakfast, then Philadelphia to Boston before lunch; two blue-eyed parents with a brown-eyed baby. ([PRH Author News, 2015-11](https://authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/maintaining-continuity-tales-from-the-copy-editor/))
- Fiction style sheet sections, synthesised from the PRH and EFA sources above:
  - Reference choices: dictionary and style manual, and spelling variety (US or UK).
  - Numbers: words or figures, times, dates.
  - Punctuation: serial comma, dashes, ellipses, quotation marks, how thoughts are styled, how text messages are styled.
  - Capitalisation and hyphenation word list.
  - Characters: name variants, nicknames, age, eye and hair colour, relationships, job, speech tics.
  - Places, including layouts.
  - Timeline: day by day, with seasons and times of day.
  - Invented terms and world rules.
  - POV and tense choices.
  - Deliberate non-standard usages. Mark these "author style, keep".
- The story bible is the book-level memory. It holds the style sheet's character and place sections plus plot facts ("who knows what by when"), objects, and open threads (setups waiting for payoff). Same sources.

### 2.4 Passes

- Order: structure, then line, then copy, then proof. (CIEP PDF, p. 4)
- Copyeditors work in one pass or several, depending on budget and how messy the manuscript is. In trade publishing, 2 to 3 copyedit rounds are reported, about 9 to 11 months before publication. These are secondary sources and unverified. ([Catherine Forrest, Copyediting Deep Dive II](https://catherineforrest.substack.com/p/copyediting-deep-dive-part-ii))
- A common technique: on the first pass, build the style sheet and record decisions without fixing. Then make a mechanical pass against the sheet. (secondary, same search; unverified)
- CIEP: "One pair of eyes is not enough." The copyeditor and the proofreader should be different people. ([CIEP FAQ](https://www.ciep.uk/resource/what-is-the-difference-between-copyediting-and-proofreading.html))

## 3. Fiction craft lenses: what is current and what to ask

Each lens below gives its status in 2026, any criticism, and the diagnostic questions an editor can ask.

### 3.1 John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (1983)

- **Core ideas.** Fiction as a "vivid and continuous dream". Profluence, the sense that the story is moving. Faults that break the dream: clumsy prose, needless explanation, sentimentality, mannerism, frigidity, and careless shifts in psychic distance. ([Kirkus review, 1983](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/john-gardner/the-art-of-fiction/); [Yale Teachers Institute unit](https://teachersinstitute.yale.edu/curriculum/units/2011/1/11.01.01/3)) Penguin Random House sold the error chapter as a 24-page ebook, *Common Errors*, in 2019. ([PRH](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/635520/common-errors-by-john-gardner/9780593082195))
- **Psychic distance ladder.** Five rungs, from far to close:
  1. "It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway."
  2. "Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms."
  3. "Henry hated snowstorms."
  4. "God how he hated these damn snowstorms."
  5. "Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul..."

  ([UNR Writing Center](https://www.unr.edu/writing-speaking-center/student-resources/writing-speaking-resources/psychic-distance-in-creative-writing); [Jericho Writers](https://jerichowriters.com/narrative-distance-definition-with-examples-for-fiction-writers/))
- **Status in 2026.** Still the standard vocabulary. Emma Darwin's 2024 post says an unsuccessful distance choice often signals a deeper narrative problem. ([Emma Darwin, 2024-02-02](https://emmadarwin.substack.com/p/itchy-bitesized-29-six-thoughts-about))
- **Criticism.** Blake Butler argues the continuous-dream rule restricts deliberately discontinuous or reader-addressing work. ([Butler, 2025-02-18](https://blakebutler.substack.com/p/the-rules-are-myths-4-undoing-gardners)) Do not flag deliberate metafiction as a dream-break.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Where does the distance jump more than one rung without a bridge?
  - Which sentence wakes the reader from the dream, and why?
  - Is the narration explaining what the scene already showed?
  - Does each paragraph move the story forward?

### 3.2 Dwight Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965); Jack Bickham, *Scene & Structure*

- **Model.** A scene is goal, conflict, disaster. A sequel is reaction, dilemma, decision, and the decision sets the next goal. The motivation-reaction unit (MRU) alternates an external stimulus with the POV character's response, in order. ([Wikipedia, Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel); [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/scene-and-sequel/))
- **Bickham.** His *Scene & Structure* (1993) popularised Swain's model. This is from memory and unverified this session.
- **Status in 2026.** Still taught in genre fiction. It is a poor fit for literary or quiet fiction, so treat it as a lens, not a law. That caution is my judgement.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - What does the POV character want in this scene?
  - What opposes it?
  - Does the scene end worse, or differently, for them?
  - Where is the reaction to the last setback?
  - Does a choice launch the next goal?
  - Are MRUs in order (stimulus before response), or does the character react before the cause appears?

### 3.3 Robert McKee, *Story* (1997)

- **Model.** A scene is "action through conflict" that turns a value-charged condition in a character's life. Note the value's charge (+ or -) at the start and at the end. If nothing turns, the scene is a "nonevent". The gap between what a character expects and what happens drives the drama. ([McKee, "Do your scenes turn?"](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/))
- **Status in 2026.** Still widely cited by screenwriters and by Story Grid, which builds on it.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Which value is at stake, and what is its charge at the start and the end?
  - Where is the turning point?
  - What did the character expect, and what came back instead?

### 3.4 Story Grid (Shawn Coyne, 2015 onward)

- **Five commandments.** These apply at every scale: inciting incident, progressive complication (turning point), crisis (a dilemma between best bad choices or irreconcilable goods), climax (the choice and the action), resolution. ([Story Grid, 5 Commandments](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/))
- **Editor's six core questions.** ([Story Grid](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/))
  1. "What's the genre?"
  2. "What are the conventions and obligatory scenes for that genre?"
  3. "What's the point of view?"
  4. "What are the objects of desire?"
  5. "What's the controlling idea/theme?"
  6. "What is the beginning hook, the middle build and the ending payoff?"
- **Foolscap Global Story Grid.** One page in four parts: global story (genre and expectations), beginning hook, middle build, ending payoff. Each part notes external and internal value shifts. ([Story Grid, Foolscap](https://storygrid.com/the-foolscap-story-grid/))
- **The Story Grid spreadsheet.** One row per scene, tracking value shift, polarity, turning point, POV, time, location and characters on stage. These column names come from memory and earlier Story Grid material, and are unverified this session.
- **Status in 2026.** Active. It runs a certified-editor programme. Story Grid Press closed to outside fiction submissions, and a "State of Story Grid" event was held on 2025-11-13. ([search results, storygrid.com/cert](https://storygrid.com/cert/); unverified detail)
- **Criticism.** Its jargon and genre taxonomy can feel formulaic for literary work. This is general opinion, unverified.

### 3.5 John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007)

- **Seven key steps.** Weakness and need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium. The 22-step version fills in the middle. ([truby.com](https://truby.com/the-anatomy-of-story/); [Sci-Fi London review](https://news.sci-fi-london.com/news/books/2010/07/anatomy-story))
- **Questions to ask:**
  - What is the hero's psychological need, as distinct from what they say they want?
  - Is the opponent fighting for the same goal?
  - Does the self-revelation answer the weakness set up at the start?

### 3.6 Lisa Cron, *Wired for Story* (2012) and *Story Genius* (2016)

- **Model.** Story is about how the protagonist changes internally. A plot exists to force a "misbelief" or inner issue into the open.
- **Criticism.** Reviewers call the brain-science framing reductive or marketing. One review says the promised "secret blueprint" is "good, classic writing advice repackaged in a trendy wrapper". ([the-agency-review.com on Cron](https://the-agency-review.com/cron/), via search snippet; unverified in full) Use the craft questions. Never cite the neuroscience as fact.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - What does the protagonist believe at the start that is wrong?
  - Does each scene test that belief?
  - Does the reader know why events matter to this person?

### 3.7 Renni Browne and Dave King, *Self-Editing for Fiction Writers* (2nd ed., 2004; first published 1993)

- **Chapters.** Show and Tell; Characterization and Exposition; Point of View; Proportion; Dialogue Mechanics; See How It Sounds; Interior Monologue; Easy Beats; Breaking Up Is Easy To Do; Once Is (Usually) Enough; Sophistication; Voice. Each chapter ends with a checklist. ([The Editorial Department](https://editorialdepartment.com/self-editing-for-fiction-writers/))
- **Status in 2026.** Still the standard line-level fiction checklist. The 2004 date for the second edition is from memory; the publisher page gives no year.
- **Known slogans.** "RUE" (resist the urge to explain) and a preference for "said". These are from memory and only partly confirmed by the page's "explanations" line.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Is this told where it should be shown, or shown where a line of telling would serve better?
  - Is there an exposition dump?
  - Is the proportion off, with too many words on minor things?
  - Are there fancy dialogue tags or adverbs?
  - Are there too many beats, or none?
  - Is a word, gesture or idea repeated without intent?
  - Is interior monologue tagged in a way that breaks the voice?

### 3.8 George Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain* (2021)

- **Method.** Read your draft "the way a first-time reader might ('without hope and without despair')". Watch an imagined meter with "P" (positive) and "N" (negative) needles. Make small changes that move it toward P, across many drafts. ([Longreads, 2017](https://longreads.com/2017/03/19/how-a-story-becomes-a-hopeful-thing-george-saunders-on-his-writing-process/))
- **In the book.** Stop after each page and summarise what you know, what you are curious about, and where the story is going. "Always be escalating." ([secondary: A Faster No](https://afasterno.substack.com/p/writing-lessons-from-the-russians); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Swim_in_a_Pond_in_the_Rain))
- **Status in 2026.** Active through his Story Club on Substack, including a revision post dated 2026-01-04. That post is paywalled and unread. ([Story Club](https://georgesaunders.substack.com/p/a-revision-exercise))
- **Questions to ask (these suit an AI reader well):**
  - Page by page: what does the reader now know?
  - What do they expect next?
  - Did the story meet or beat that expectation, or just repeat it?
  - Where did the needle drop, and at which sentence?
  - Does each section escalate?

### 3.9 Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (revised 2015)

- **Chapters.** The Sound of Your Writing; Punctuation and Grammar; Sentence Length and Complex Syntax; Repetition; Adjectives and Adverbs; Verbs: Person and Tense; Point of View and Voice; Changing Point of View; Indirect Narration, or What Tells; Crowding and Leaping. ([library record](https://search.icpl.org/Record/1461335); [NPR, 2015-08-29](https://www.npr.org/2015/08/29/435549081/ursula-k-le-guin-steers-her-craft-into-a-new-century))
- **Crowding and leaping.** Crowding means keeping prose "interconnected with itself, rich with echoes forward and backward". Leaping means "What you leave out is infinitely more than what you leave in." This is the best compression lens for short fiction. ([radacts substack quoting the book](https://radacts.substack.com/p/crowding-and-leaping))
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Read it aloud. Where does the sound snag?
  - Is repetition deliberate (an echo) or accidental?
  - Does a POV change happen on purpose, and is it signalled?
  - Which transitions could leap instead of walk?

### 3.10 Other sources, verified

- **Donald Maass, *Writing the Breakout Novel* (2001).** "Tension on every page". Microtension, meaning conflicting emotions inside the POV character, line by line. He develops this further in *The Fire in Fiction* and *Writing 21st Century Fiction*, and treats emotion in *The Emotional Craft of Fiction*. ([PRH](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/isbn/9781582971827); [Janet Fox on microtension](https://janetfox.substack.com/p/what-is-micro-tension)) **Question:** on a slow page, what does the POV character feel that pulls two ways?
- **Matt Bell, *Refuse to Be Done* (2022).** Three drafts. After the first, reverse outline the draft into a "narrative outline". Retype the second draft from scratch, with no cut and paste. Polish the third with a checklist of one-task passes. Bell borrows "rate of revelation" from Jim Shepard. ([Poets & Writers](https://www.pw.org/node/1138236); [Dead Darlings](https://www.deaddarlings.com/ten-tips-i-learned-about-novel-revision-from-matt-bell-author-of-refuse-to-be-done/)) **Question:** what does the reader learn in each chapter, and is the rate even?
- **Jane Alison, *Meander, Spiral, Explode* (2019).** Patterns other than the arc: wave, meander, spiral, radial or explosion, fractal, cell. ([Chicago Review of Books, 2019-04-03](https://chireviewofbooks.com/2019/04/03/instead-of-climaxing-novels-should-meander-spiral-explode/)) **Rule:** before flagging "no arc", check whether the piece runs on another pattern, and judge it by that pattern.
- **Charles Baxter, *Burning Down the House* (1997) and *The Art of Subtext* (2007).** "Dysfunctional Narratives" criticises stories with no agency ("mistakes were made"). Subtext covers what is not said. ([Graywolf/Macmillan](https://us.macmillan.com/tradebooksforcourses/academictrade/9781555974732/theartofsubtext)) **Questions:** does anyone choose anything? What is the scene really about beneath the talk?
- **Jennie Nash and Author Accelerator, "Inside Outline" from *Blueprint for a Book* (2021).** For each scene, write what happens (plot) and why it matters to the protagonist (point). Link scenes with "because of that", not "and then". Keep it to at most 3 pages, with at most 3 lines per bullet. ([Inside Outline workbook](https://marketing.prowritingaid.com/The-Inside-Outline-Workbook.pdf); [Author Accelerator course](https://authoraccelerator.teachable.com/courses/392440)) This is a strong test of causality.
- **Fictionary.** 38 story elements in three groups: character, plot and setting. Each scene should have an entry hook, a POV goal, a scene middle, a scene climax and an exit hook. ([Fictionary, 2024-04-29](https://fictionary.co/journal/story-elements); [scene structure](https://fictionary.co/journal/scene-structure-evaluation)) The full list of 38 sits inside the product and is not enumerated publicly.
- Not verified this session and left out on purpose: Sol Stein, Kristen Kieffer.

## 4. Short stories

### 4.1 Length bands

| Band | Words | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Flash | Usually under 1,000; some markets 500, some 1,500 | [Flash Fiction Online](https://flashfictiononline.com/how-to-write-flash-fiction/how-to-write-flash-fiction-definition) (500 to 1,000 for them) |
| Short story | Under 7,500 | [SFWA Nebula rules](https://nebulas.sfwa.org/nebula-rules/) |
| Novelette | 7,500 to under 17,500 | same |
| Novella | 17,500 to under 40,000 | same |
| Novel | 40,000 and up | same |

Markets set their own limits. Clarkesworld takes 1,000 to 22,000 words, "no exceptions". It bans AI-assisted submissions: "translated, written, developed, or assisted by these tools" may get the writer banned. ([Clarkesworld submissions](https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/submissions/)) An editwright pass must therefore never produce text that the author submits as their own to such markets. This is a strong reason for the plugin's diagnose-only stance.

### 4.2 Why slush readers reject

- **The Writer** (Aigner Loren Wilson, 2024-07-12) lists seven reasons ([The Writer](https://www.writermag.com/?p=56828)):
  - long-winded openings with backstory before the action
  - endings disconnected from the story's promises
  - no emotional beats
  - a main character who is not active
  - the wrong market
  - cliché topics
  - "nothing happens", meaning no change
- **Clarkesworld "hard sells".** Among them: undead, standard vampire or werewolf tropes, sentient animals or weapons, children finding significant objects outdoors, RPG adventures, pun-driven humour, and protagonists defined only by intelligence or eccentricity. ([Clarkesworld](https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/submissions/))
- **Slush practice.** Readers often stop within the first page. Common opening faults: paragraphs of weather, room or furniture description; first sentences overloaded with "stuff". ([search synthesis incl. Apex Magazine "Why do you keep rejecting me"](https://www.apexbookcompany.com/blogs/apex-magazine/why-do-you-keep-rejecting-me): Apex returned 429, so this is unverified in full)

### 4.3 Compression rules for short fiction

These are synthesised from Le Guin, Saunders and The Writer. They are judgement, not quotes.

- One central change. Every scene serves it.
- Start as late as the reader can follow. Backstory goes in by the clause, not the page.
- Every early detail should be paid off or should colour the end (crowding). Cut the transitions that walk (leaping).
- The ending answers the question the opening raised. It may answer it obliquely, but it must answer it.
- In flash, the title and the last line carry more weight. Check both.

## 5. Books: manuscript-level work

- **Reverse outline first.** Make one line per scene or chapter, from the draft as written, not as intended. (Matt Bell's narrative outline; Nash's Inside Outline uses the same idea, applied after the fact.)
- **Scene list or spreadsheet.** One row per scene with these columns:
  - chapter and scene, word count, POV, time and day, location
  - characters on stage
  - goal, conflict and outcome (Swain)
  - value at start and end, turning point (McKee, Story Grid)
  - what the reader learns (Bell's rate of revelation)
  - setups and payoffs
  - entry and exit hooks (Fictionary)

  The columns are synthesised from the sources in section 3.
- **What the sheet reveals:**
  - POV imbalance
  - sagging middles, meaning runs of scenes with no value turn
  - timeline impossibilities
  - characters who vanish
  - duplicate scenes that do the same job
- **Beat sheet.** Compare the scene list with the genre's expected beats and obligatory scenes (Story Grid questions 1 and 2). Report missing beats as questions, not as orders.
- **Story bible and continuity log.** Build it on the first read and update it every pass. It is the agent's memory across chapters, which matters most when the context window cannot hold the whole book. Use the PRH copy editor's habit: a running note of chronology, logistics, plot and personal details, plus a separate timeline.
- **Order for a full manuscript:**
  1. Read through without marking. Take bible notes only.
  2. Reverse outline and scene sheet.
  3. Developmental letter.
  4. *Author revises.*
  5. Line pass.
  6. Copy pass, with the style sheet built on its first sweep.
  7. Proof on laid-out pages.

  This follows CIEP's order. Steps 4 and 6 come from practitioner sources in section 2.4.

## 6. Diagnostic checklists per level

Every item is a question. editwright reports findings with locations and options, and the author writes. Sources are in brackets.

### 6.1 Manuscript assessment (critique)

- [ ] Can I state premise, genre and intended reader in three sentences? If not, that is finding 1. [Story Grid Q1](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/)
- [ ] Does the work deliver the conventions and obligatory scenes of its genre? [Story Grid Q2](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/)
- [ ] Beginning hook, middle build, ending payoff: does each exist and turn? [Story Grid Q6](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/)
- [ ] What does the protagonist want, what do they need, and are the two different? [Truby](https://truby.com/the-anatomy-of-story/)
- [ ] Is there a choice at the crisis, or does the ending just happen? [Story Grid](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/); [Baxter](https://us.macmillan.com/tradebooksforcourses/academictrade/9781555974732/theartofsubtext)
- [ ] Top three strengths and top three issues, with page references. Keep the report under about 10 pages and make no line edits. [Friedman/Chauncey](https://janefriedman.com/manuscript-evaluations-what-they-are-and-what-to-expect/)

### 6.2 Developmental (structural)

- [ ] Reverse outline: does each scene cause the next ("because of that")? [Nash](https://marketing.prowritingaid.com/The-Inside-Outline-Workbook.pdf)
- [ ] Does each scene turn a value? Flag non-events. [McKee](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)
- [ ] Goal, conflict, disaster; reaction, dilemma, decision: present where the genre expects them? [Swain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)
- [ ] POV: whose story is it, are POV changes deliberate, and is the psychic distance controlled? [Gardner](https://www.unr.edu/writing-speaking-center/student-resources/writing-speaking-resources/psychic-distance-in-creative-writing); [Le Guin](https://www.npr.org/2015/08/29/435549081/ursula-k-le-guin-steers-her-craft-into-a-new-century)
- [ ] Rate of revelation: what does the reader learn per chapter, and where does it stall? [Bell](https://www.pw.org/node/1138236)
- [ ] Does each scene have an entry hook and an exit hook? [Fictionary](https://fictionary.co/journal/scene-structure-evaluation)
- [ ] Is the protagonist active, and is there emotional reaction to events? [The Writer](https://www.writermag.com/?p=56828)
- [ ] Before calling the shape broken, check whether it is a non-arc pattern (spiral, wave, meander). [Alison](https://chireviewofbooks.com/2019/04/03/instead-of-climaxing-novels-should-meander-spiral-explode/)
- [ ] Deliver a letter with prioritised issues and two or more options each, plus margin notes. Do not rewrite. [EFA](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/); [CIEP](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)

### 6.3 Line

- [ ] Told where shown would serve, or shown where one line of telling would serve better? [Browne & King ch. 1](https://editorialdepartment.com/self-editing-for-fiction-writers/)
- [ ] Explanation of what the scene already showed (dream-breaking)? [Gardner](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/john-gardner/the-art-of-fiction/)
- [ ] Unbridged jumps in psychic distance within a paragraph? [Gardner](https://jerichowriters.com/narrative-distance-definition-with-examples-for-fiction-writers/)
- [ ] Dialogue mechanics: ornate tags, adverbs, too many or too few beats? [Browne & King ch. 5 and 8](https://editorialdepartment.com/self-editing-for-fiction-writers/)
- [ ] Unintended repetition of words, gestures or ideas? [Browne & King ch. 10](https://editorialdepartment.com/self-editing-for-fiction-writers/); [Le Guin](https://search.icpl.org/Record/1461335)
- [ ] Microtension: on slow pages, is there an inner pull? [Maass](https://janetfox.substack.com/p/what-is-micro-tension)
- [ ] Read aloud: rhythm and sentence-length monotony? [Le Guin](https://search.icpl.org/Record/1461335)
- [ ] Page-by-page P/N: where does a first-time reader's interest drop? [Saunders](https://longreads.com/2017/03/19/how-a-story-becomes-a-hopeful-thing-george-saunders-on-his-writing-process/)
- [ ] Every suggestion is offered, not imposed, and keeps the author's diction. Never change voice unless it fails the intended readership. [CIEP p. 6](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)

### 6.4 Copy

- [ ] Style sheet built first: references, numbers, punctuation, word list, characters, places, timeline, world terms, deliberate deviations. [PRH](https://authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/maintaining-continuity-tales-from-the-copy-editor/); [EFA](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/)
- [ ] Spelling, grammar, usage and punctuation corrected against the sheet. [EFA](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/)
- [ ] Continuity: ages, eye colour, travel times, days of the week, who knows what. [PRH](https://authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/maintaining-continuity-tales-from-the-copy-editor/)
- [ ] Dialogue and thought styling follow CMOS 18 fiction guidance, or the author's stated style. [CMOS What's New](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/help-tools/what-s-new.html)
- [ ] Query obvious factual errors; do no research. Flag possible legal issues (libel, copyright); do not solve them. [CIEP p. 17](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)
- [ ] Queries are valid, clear, concise, humble, numbered and polite. [ACES](https://aceseditors.org/news/2021/author-queries-for-the-nonfiction-editor-how-to-raise-em-and-how-to-phrase-em)
- [ ] No substantial rewriting. [CIEP p. 17](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)

### 6.5 Proofread

- [ ] Typos, layout errors, and style decisions applied correctly. [CIEP p. 16](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)
- [ ] Earlier corrections actually made (compare stages). [EFA](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/)
- [ ] Figures and tables numbered and placed; index formatted consistently. [CIEP p. 16](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)
- [ ] Out of scope: rephrasing for flow, changing spelling preferences, restructuring. [CIEP p. 16](https://www.ciep.uk/static/bc4737d3-d680-4c37-af1138094c64427d/CIEP-Proofreading-or-Editing-free-ebook.pdf)

### 6.6 Fact-check

- [ ] List every assertion of fact. Give each a verdict (confirmed, wrong, unverifiable) and a source. [EFA](https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/)
- [ ] In fiction: real places, dates, technology, law, medicine and period details. Flag invented elements only where the text implies they are real. (judgement)

### 6.7 Sensitivity and authenticity read

- [ ] Note tropes, stereotypes, inaccuracies and offensive portrayals. Also note good representation. [SFWA](https://sfwa.org/2024/04/02/what-is-sensitivity-reading/)
- [ ] An AI is not a lived-experience reader. editwright may flag candidate passages and recommend a human reader. It must not pose as one. (judgement; the EFA and SFWA both define the role by community membership or lived experience)
- [ ] The author decides every change. [SFWA](https://sfwa.org/2024/04/02/what-is-sensitivity-reading/)

### 6.8 Short story extras

- [ ] Fits the target market's band, using SFWA bands and the market's own limits. [SFWA](https://nebulas.sfwa.org/nebula-rules/); [Clarkesworld](https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/submissions/)
- [ ] First 500 words: does something happen to someone, or is it setting and backstory? [The Writer](https://www.writermag.com/?p=56828)
- [ ] One central change; the ending pays off the opening's promise. [The Writer](https://www.writermag.com/?p=56828)
