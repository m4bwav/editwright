# Editorial deliverables

## Editorial letter
Structure, synthesised from Scott Norton, *Developmental Editing*, 2nd ed. (U. Chicago Press, 2023) and practitioner pages ([editing craft note](../../../ai-docs/research/2026-10-08-editing-craft.md)):
1. What the book is: one paragraph on premise, genre and intended reader. Proves the editor understood it.
2. Strengths, specific, with locations.
3. Big issues in priority order, usually three to five. Each: symptom with examples, why it costs the reader, two or more options. The author chooses.
4. Element notes: structure, character, POV, pacing, setting, dialogue, voice, genre conventions.
5. Revision plan: an order of operations.
6. Answers to the author's own questions.

Length: an assessment "usually no more than 10 pages"; a developmental letter can pass 30 pages with hundreds of comments (janefriedman.com/manuscript-evaluations-what-they-are-and-what-to-expect). Vendor pages say 5 to 10 pages (unverified). Margin comments point to the letter ("see letter, issue 2"); they do not repeat it.

## Margin comments and author queries
- "AU:" addresses the author. A publishing convention, not a Chicago rule (crosstalk.cell.com).
- ACES essentials: tone, specificity, tact (aceseditors.org, 2019). "Revise." and "Huh?" fail. A good query names what is wrong and why, and offers a direction.
- ACES seven rules: valid, clear, concise, humble, efficient, polite, selective. A query is not valid if it forces the editor's preference or silences the author's voice (aceseditors.org, 2021).
- Option form: say you are unsure what is meant, offer two readings plus "or something else", ask the author to advise.
- Number queries serially, group by chapter, log date and answer, accept rejections gracefully (same ACES page).
- CIEP voice principle: do not change tone of voice or style unless it is wrong for the readership. "Very few rights and wrongs"; consistency, clarity and context are key; know a rule from a preference (CIEP PDF pp. 5-6).
- Change or query: replace only what is objectively wrong and covered by the agreed style (typo, style-sheet spelling, punctuation convention). Query anything touching meaning, voice, fact or a choice that looks deliberate (synthesis of ACES and CIEP). In editwright, anything needing new wording is always a query.
- Fiction practice: query more, offer rephrasing options rather than rewrites (secondary, unverified).

## Style sheet (copyedit deliverable, EFA)
- References: dictionary, style manual, US or UK spelling.
- Numbers: words or figures, times, dates.
- Punctuation: serial comma, dashes, ellipses, quote marks, how thoughts and text messages are styled.
- Word list: capitalisation and hyphenation.
- Characters: name variants, nicknames, age, eye and hair colour, relationships, job, speech tics.
- Places, including layouts.
- Timeline: day by day, seasons, times of day.
- Invented terms and world rules.
- POV and tense choices.
- Deliberate non-standard usages, marked "author style, keep".

Sources: EFA definitions (the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions); a Penguin Random House copy editor's practice (authornews.penguinrandomhouse.com/maintaining-continuity-tales-from-the-copy-editor), who delivers a style sheet of every proper noun plus odd words and a separate timeline.

## Story bible and continuity log
- Bible: the style sheet's character and place sections, plus plot facts ("who knows what by when"), objects, open threads (setups awaiting payoff). Build on the first read; update every pass. It is the agent's memory across chapters.
- Continuity log: each conflict with both locations quoted; status open or resolved by the author.
- Typical catches (PRH): breakfast, then Philadelphia to Boston before lunch; two blue-eyed parents with a brown-eyed baby.

## Scene spreadsheet
One row per scene, columns synthesised from the craft lenses:
- chapter and scene, word count, POV, time and day, location
- characters on stage
- goal, conflict, outcome (Swain)
- value at start and end, turning point (McKee, Story Grid)
- what the reader learns (Bell's rate of revelation)
- setups and payoffs
- entry and exit hooks (Fictionary)

It reveals POV imbalance, sagging middles (runs with no value turn), timeline impossibilities, vanishing characters and duplicate scenes doing the same job. Compare against the genre's expected beats and report missing beats as questions.
