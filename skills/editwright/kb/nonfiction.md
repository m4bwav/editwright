# Nonfiction

Source: [scripts, nonfiction and books note](../../../ai-docs/research/2026-10-08-scripts-nonfiction-books.md), section 2.

## Argument check (diagnose only)
Vocabulary:
- Toulmin (owl.purdue.edu, Toulmin Argument): claim; grounds (evidence); warrant (the assumption linking grounds to claim); optional backing, qualifier, rebuttal.
- Graff and Birkenstein, *They Say / I Say*, 6th ed. (Norton, 2024): name the view you answer, then your own.
- Booth, Colomb and Williams, *The Craft of Research*, 5th ed. (U. Chicago Press, 2024): claim, reasons, evidence, acknowledgment and response, warrant.

Steps:
1. State the piece's claim in one sentence. If you cannot, that is the first note.
2. Per section: claim, grounds, warrant. Flag missing warrants and grounds that support a different claim.
3. Flag claims stronger than their evidence; suggest that a qualifier is needed, without writing it.
4. Is the "they say" stated fairly before it is answered? Flag straw men.
5. Name the strongest objection the text ignores.

Book level: state the reader promise from the introduction; check each chapter delivers part of it and the end pays it off. One job per chapter, an opening that sets the question, evidence, a close that answers and hands off. Flag repeated material. Structure before chapter arguments before lines; never line-edit a chapter that may be cut. Reverse outline per paragraph (what it says, what it does) shows overloaded paragraphs, thin ideas and order problems (writingcenter.virginia.edu/reverse-outlining-organization-strategy).

## Fact-check: flag, never assert
- Never call a fact true or false from model memory. Generative search tools gave wrong answers on over 60% of news-citation queries (Tow Center, cjr.org, March 2025, via search).
- What gets checked: names, titles, dates, numbers, quotations, descriptions or interpretations of data (Borel, *The Chicago Guide to Fact-Checking*, 2nd ed., 2023).
- Claim table columns: `# | location | claim (quoted) | type | source given in text | risk | status`.
- Types: name, number, date, quote, place, title, statistic, causal claim.
- Risk: legal, high, normal. Legal first: named living people, crimes, health, money.
- Status: `unchecked`, `source given`, `source given, not matching`, `checked by user`, `checked against <URL, date>`.
- Use `checked against` only when a primary source was fetched in this session; quote the supporting line.
- Flag every direct quote for checking against the recording or document. Never fix a quote's wording.
- Internal arithmetic (totals, percentages, units) is not a fact claim; check it.

## Style guide choice
| Guide | Current edition | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Chicago | 18th, September 2024 | books, trade and academic, fiction too |
| AP Stylebook | 58th, released 2026-08-01 | news, PR, corporate web copy |
| APA | 7th (2019); no 8th announced | psychology, social sciences, education, nursing |
| MLA | 9th (2021); no 10th announced | literature, languages, humanities papers |

Sources: tamu.libguides.com/chicago; mastheadonline.com (2026-08-01); apa.org Publication Manual page; MLA via library guides. Pages claiming an "APA 8th edition" are spam or wrong.

Notes: Chicago 18 adds inclusive-language and accessibility review, fiction and self-publishing scope, AI citation, and asks authors to say whether and how far AI was used (4.51). AP 58 has 200+ new or revised entries, a revised AI chapter and closed compounds such as healthcare. AP 57 moved to Merriam-Webster.

Choosing:
1. The publisher's or outlet's house style wins. Ask.
2. Otherwise by venue: news and web, AP; book, Chicago; course or journal, the discipline's guide.
3. Record the choice and every decision in the style sheet at the start (spellings, capitalisation, numbers, serial comma). Check consistency against it; never impose a guide the author did not choose.
4. Do not quote the paid guides at length. Cite rule numbers only when the user supplies them or a free official page states them.

## Proposal (developmental)
A proposal is a business case: hook, overview, audience and market, comparable titles, bio and platform, marketing plan, chapter outline, sample chapters. Platform often outweighs prose for nonfiction (janefriedman.com/book-proposal).
