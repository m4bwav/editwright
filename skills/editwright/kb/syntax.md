# Syntax checks for the line pass

For any genre, fiction included. Each item is something to read for and raise as a query or the smallest reorder that reuses the author's words ([ai-text-rules.md](ai-text-rules.md)); never a rewrite. Fiction voice and dialogue may break these on purpose: check the style sheet's "author style, keep" entries first. Book rules, unverified against readers. Source: Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), ch. 4 and ch. 5. Word-level clarity for nonfiction: [clarity.md](clarity.md).

## Errors to check
- **Agreement across inserted phrases.** When a phrase or a relative clause sits between subject and verb, the noun next to the verb may not be the subject. Find the head noun and check the verb against it.
- **Who and whom.** Put the word back where it came from in the clause and try he or him in that spot.
- **Paired markers.** Both with and, either with or, neither with nor, and the not-only pair: what follows each marker should be the same kind of phrase. Each branch of any coordination should also work alone in that slot.
- **Pronoun case in pairs.** "Between you and I" and "me and her went": delete the other half of the pair and see which case sounds right.

## Hard to parse
- **Garden paths.** A sentence that sends the reader down a wrong reading before it resolves. The writer cannot see them; reading aloud, or a cold reread, finds them. Remedies to suggest: a comma, a kept "that" or "the", parallel structure, or moving a phrase away from a neighbour it could wrongly attach to.
- **Misattached modifiers.** Readers attach a phrase to the nearest candidate. Ask whether a phrase could attach to the wrong word, and suggest pulling unrelated phrases apart rather than only keeping related ones together.
- **Centre-embedding.** A clause nested inside a clause inside a clause, with verbs piling up at the end, is the usual cause of "convoluted". Suggest unnesting each clause beside the one it sat in, then splitting into sentences.
- **Long left branches.** Many words before the main subject, or a stack of four or more nouns used as modifiers, make the reader hold everything before knowing what it is about.
- **Needless variation.** Repeating a structure lets the reader reuse the parse; switching it for variety misleads, above all in comparisons, where the two halves should match word for word.

## Order of information
- **Heavy last.** Put the longest phrase at the end of a list or a clause.
- **Known before new.** What the reader already has goes first, the news last. A passive, a different verb (give or receive, buy or sell) or another reordering may get there; a passive chosen for this is a good passive.
- **Mention order and event order.** Before, after and preposed time clauses can tell events out of order; check the reader can recover the real sequence.
- **Negation.** Deny only what a reader might believe; otherwise ask for the plain affirmative. Three or more negatives in one sentence often end up meaning the opposite. Put "not" and "only" next to the words they limit.
- **One connective per relation.** "The reason is because" and "so therefore" mark one link twice.

## Not a finding
- A long sentence that branches right or runs flat reads easily; do not flag length alone.
- Cutting words that stop a wrong turn makes the sentence worse. Shorter is a value, not a fact (Cameron, in [line-copy-proof.md](line-copy-proof.md)).
