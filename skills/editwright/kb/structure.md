# Document architecture

The level between the whole book and the sentence: how sections, blocks and paragraphs tell the reader where they are. Each item is a flag and a question for the author; editwright never drafts the missing introduction or map. Book rules, unverified against readers. Source: Armstrong and Terrell, *Thinking Like a Writer* (PLI, 2009), ch. 2 to 5, 7, 8 and 14. Argument checks: [nonfiction.md](nonfiction.md). Word-level clarity: [clarity.md](clarity.md).

## Context before detail
- **A short introduction before every block of detail**, not only at the start of the document and its sections. It says what the block bears on (a question, a conclusion or a topic), how it is built, and how it links back to what came before (ch. 2, ch. 3).
- Three faults to flag: the introduction is missing, it comes after the detail it explains, or it is too vague to steer ("several cases discuss X" instead of what the cases turn on) (ch. 3).
- A section that opens with a run of dated facts and no word on why they matter is the named case (ch. 2).
- How explicit to be depends on the reader: less expert or less patient readers, and harder material, need more (ch. 2).
- Skim tests: read only the headings, then only the paragraph openings. Does each set still give the argument? (ch. 3, ch. 8)

## Maps and roadsigns
- **A map** is a sentence that gives the count and names the parts before the first one, even for two or three parts (ch. 3).
- **Four items or fewer.** Above four, ask whether the items group under a few headings, each with its own map later. Book rule, unverified: the authors' experience, not a measured limit (ch. 3).
- **Roadsigns repeat the map.** Each part opens with the map's own words, in the map's order: a numeral, "second", or a heading. Varying the wording for elegance breaks the link (ch. 3).
- A singular "the reason is" followed later by "Furthermore" hides a second reason; ask for the count up front (ch. 2).

The ways a map goes wrong (ch. 3):
- **Too faint.** Readers miss it; the count and the parts are not stated plainly.
- **Missing** because the material is "too interconnected"; the writer still has to pick an order.
- **Inadvertent map.** Any list near a section's start reads as its outline. If the sections then differ in order or wording, flag it.
- **Distant roadsign.** By part two the heading uses other words than the map did.
- **Conflicting structure.** A section's own order fights the document's map.
- **Hidden hierarchy.** A flat list of four that is really one, two-a, two-b, three; or three points in one paragraph and a fourth alone, which makes the fourth look as big as the rest together (ch. 2, ch. 4).

## Default orders
Each is a flag only when nothing in the piece justifies it (ch. 4):
- **The research path.** The order the writer found things. Fair when teaching a method, or when the conclusion is so contested it must be approached slowly.
- **The opponent's outline.** Answering their points in their order cedes the frame. Ask whether the writer's own framework should come first.
- **Authorities by date.** Fair only when each case builds on the last; otherwise by the fact that separates them.
- **The flat list.** May hide sub-points or an if-then link between items.
- **Facts by date.** Chronology can stand in for a theme and sweep in every fact in range. Ask which facts the analysis uses, and whether a theme order would show a missing one.

## Legal and business documents
- **Memo order.** Short facts, the question, a summary conclusion, then the analysis. No closing wrap-up that repeats the summary. A memo that departs from this should have a reason (ch. 14).
- **The summary conclusion** answers the practical problem as well as the legal one, gives the likelihood, and puts each answer after its question when there are several. Test: could the senior reader act, or call the client, on it alone? (ch. 7, ch. 14)
- **Full point up front.** The question as the reader frames it, the answer and its likelihood, a short reason, and what the reader can do (ch. 7).
- **Before a case is discussed,** say why it matters here, then what it held (ch. 8, ch. 14).
- **Issue statements.** Terse, imply their answer, name the legal issue and the standard of review, carry the key facts, told from the client's side; court rules win (ch. 14).
- **Email.** The subject names the ask or the deadline; the bottom line sits in the first sentence or two; give day, date and time zone, not "tonight" (ch. 14).
- Headings in all capitals, or a paragraph long, go unread; ask for short headings unless a court rule binds (ch. 3, ch. 5).
- Paragraphs past about eight to ten short sentences are a place to look. Book rule, unverified (ch. 5).
