# Fiction diagnosis

Every item is a question. Report findings with locations and options; the author writes. Sources and quotes: [editing craft note](../../../ai-docs/research/2026-10-08-editing-craft.md).

## Questions by area
**POV and psychic distance** (Gardner's five rungs, far to close)
- Whose story is it? Are POV changes deliberate and signalled?
- Where does distance jump more than one rung without a bridge?
- Is interior monologue tagged in a way that breaks the voice?

**Scene and sequel** (Swain, McKee)
- What does the POV character want here, and what opposes it?
- Does the scene end worse or different for them? Which value turns, from what charge to what? No turn means a non-event.
- Where is the reaction to the last setback? Does a decision launch the next goal?
- Stimulus before response, or does the character react before the cause appears?
- Does each scene cause the next ("because of that", not "and then")?

**Stakes and desire**
- What does the protagonist want, what do they need, and do they differ? (Truby)
- What wrong belief does each scene test? (Cron; use the questions, never cite her neuroscience as fact.)
- Does anyone choose anything? Is there a choice at the crisis? (Baxter, Story Grid)

**Pacing**
- What does the reader learn per chapter, and where does it stall? (Bell)
- On a slow page, what does the POV character feel that pulls two ways? (Maass)
- Page by page: what does the reader know, expect, and did the story beat that or repeat it? Where did interest drop? Does each section escalate? (Saunders)
- Too many words on minor things? (Browne & King, proportion)

**Dialogue**
- Ornate tags or adverbs; too many beats or none? (Browne & King)
- What is the scene about beneath the talk? (Baxter, subtext)

**Character arc**
- Does the self-revelation answer the weakness set up early? (Truby)
- Is the protagonist active, with emotional reaction to events? (The Writer, 2024)

**Openings**
- Can premise, genre and reader be stated in three sentences? If not, that is finding 1. (Story Grid)
- Backstory or description before anything happens to someone? (writermag.com/?p=56828)

**Endings**
- Does the ending pay off the opening's promise and the genre's obligatory scenes? (Story Grid, The Writer)
- Is there a hook, build and payoff, each with a turn?

## Do not flag deliberate choices
- Metafiction or reader address is not a "dream break"; Gardner's continuous dream is criticised as restrictive for such work (blakebutler.substack.com, 2025-02-18).
- Before calling a shape broken, check for a non-arc pattern (wave, meander, spiral, explosion, fractal, cell) and judge it by that (Alison).
- Scene and sequel fits genre fiction poorly for literary or quiet fiction. A lens, not a law.
- Repetition may be an echo (Le Guin's crowding). Fragments and odd punctuation are often intended.
- Judge against the book's own genre and contract, never an average bestseller.

## Lenses (one line each)
- Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (1983): continuous dream, profluence, psychic distance. Standard vocabulary; criticised for restricting discontinuous work.
- Swain (1965) / Bickham (*Scene & Structure*): goal, conflict, disaster; reaction, dilemma, decision; MRUs. Taught in genre fiction; poor fit for literary. Bickham details unverified.
- McKee, *Story* (1997): scenes turn a value; expectation gap. Widely cited (mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn).
- Story Grid (Coyne, 2015 on): five commandments, editor's six questions, Foolscap. Active, certifies editors; jargon can feel formulaic for literary work. Spreadsheet columns unverified.
- Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007): seven steps from weakness and need to new equilibrium.
- Browne & King, *Self-Editing for Fiction Writers*: chapter checklists (show and tell, POV, proportion, dialogue mechanics, beats, repetition, voice). Still the standard line-level checklist.
- Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain* (2021): first-time-reader meter, page-by-page summary, escalation. Active via Story Club. Suits an AI reader.
- Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (rev. 2015): sound, repetition, POV, crowding and leaping. Best compression lens.
- Maass, *Writing the Breakout Novel* (2001): tension on every page, microtension.
- Bell, *Refuse to Be Done* (2022): reverse outline, retype, one-task polish passes, rate of revelation.
- Alison, *Meander, Spiral, Explode* (2019): patterns other than the arc.
- Nash, Inside Outline (*Blueprint for a Book*, 2021): plot and point per scene, "because of that". Strong causality test.
- Fictionary: 38 elements; per scene entry hook, POV goal, middle, climax, exit hook. Full list not public.
