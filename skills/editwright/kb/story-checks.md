# Named story checks

Each check is a flag with a location and a question for the author. None licenses a rewrite: a fix that needs new words is a query ([ai-text-rules.md](ai-text-rules.md)). Use the code as the suggestion's `category`. Book rules, unverified against readers; skip any the author's style sheet keeps on purpose.

## Developmental
- `twist-in-summary`: a main event or reveal happens in narrative summary, not in scene. (Browne & King ch. 1)
- `no-breath`: the opposite: three or more scenes in a row with no summary or transition between them. Warn only. (Browne & King ch. 1)
- `proportion-ending`: a character or place given long treatment in the first fifty pages has no weight at the end. (Browne & King ch. 4)
- `hobby-horse`: pages of the author's expertise that move no plot. (Browne & King ch. 4)
- `head-hop`: viewpoint changes inside a scene without a line space. Options: a break, or one viewpoint per scene. (Browne & King ch. 3)
- `pov-leak`: narration gives something the viewpoint character could not see or know, or would not notice in that state. (Browne & King ch. 3, ch. 4)
- `weak-want`: no desire shown for the viewpoint character in the first scene, or in the first 10% of a short story. A small want counts. (Bradbury, "The Joy of Writing")
- `outline-driven-event`: a turn caused by no character's want or fear, only by the plot's need. (Bradbury, "Zen in the Art of Writing")
- `weakness-not-moral`: at the start the hero hurts no one; the weakness is only inner. Commercial drama rule; quiet fiction may differ. (Truby ch. 8, step 3)
- `opponent-different-goal`: the main opponent does not compete for the same goal as the hero. (Truby ch. 3)
- `binary-opposition`: the cast holds only two positions on the story's moral problem. (Truby ch. 4-5)
- `reveal-no-decision`: a revelation the hero does not act on; no new plan follows. (Truby ch. 8, step 9)
- `plan-succeeds`: the hero's first plan works as made. (Truby ch. 8, step 10)
- `many-low-points`: more than one moment reads as the final defeat. (Truby ch. 8)
- `drive-repeats`: scenes in a row that leave the situation as it was. (Truby ch. 8, step 12)
- `ally-outshines`: an ally or side character more vivid or active than the hero. (Truby ch. 2, ch. 8)
- `false-choice`: the climax choice is plain good against plain evil, not two goods or two evils. (Truby ch. 2; McKee ch. 17)
- `perfect-character`: a major character with no flaw, failure or cost anywhere. (Hemingway on Writing ch. 7)
- `backstory-drift`: a character's origin, age or history told two ways. Check against the bible's backstory line per character. (Hemingway on Writing ch. 7)
- Omission: ask what a gap leaves out. If the author knows, it may be strength; if not, it is a hole. (Hemingway on Writing ch. 8)

Use Truby's seven steps for short work; never score a story against his 22.

## Line
- `stated-emotion`: narration names a feeling where its cause could carry it. Start from `EW stats` "named feelings in narration"; direct statement is sometimes right. (Browne & King ch. 1; Hemingway on Writing ch. 5)
- `ing-simultaneity`: an -ing or "as" clause joins actions that cannot overlap ("Disappearing into the tent, I changed"). Start from "-ing/as openers"; most hits are fine. Options: two verbs joined by "and", or two sentences. (Browne & King ch. 11)
- `impossible-tag`: a tag verb that cannot make speech (grimaced, smiled, chuckled a sentence). (Browne & King ch. 5)
- `name-switch`: one character called by two names in one scene. (Browne & King ch. 5)
- `stock-beat`: a beat on every line, or stock beats (window, cigarette, eyes, hands). Ask for one this character alone would do. (Browne & King ch. 8)
- `one-plus-one`: the same effect made twice in a passage; the weaker can go. (Browne & King ch. 10)
- `emphasis-crutch`: italics or exclamation marks for emphasis in narration ("! in narration" in stats). (Browne & King ch. 11)
- `climax-metaphor`: a showpiece figure at the key moment pulls the eye off it. (Browne & King ch. 11)
- White space: paragraphs over half a page (about 250 words) slow a scene; chapters all about the same length flatten rhythm. Stats lists both. (Browne & King ch. 9)

## A cut must say what survives
Every cut suggestion's `why` names what the passage does that the story still needs (a setup, a clue, the only mention of a name), checked against the bible's open threads. A cut that removes the only setup for a later payoff becomes a query. (Bradbury, interview on cutting)

On tight prose, when the author preferences mark it, a one-word change is a query: one word can unbalance a passage. (Hemingway on Writing ch. 8)
