# Clarity checks for nonfiction

Things to read for in essays, reports, articles, memos and other expository prose. Each item is a flag and a question for the author, never an automatic fix: the author writes the new wording. Book rules, unverified against readers. Source: Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), ch. 2 and ch. 3. Sentence structure for any genre: [syntax.md](syntax.md). Document structure: [structure.md](structure.md).

Do not apply these to fiction, poetry or scripts. A novel's narrator may hedge, abstract or quote on purpose.

## The checks
- **Zombie nouns.** A verb or adjective turned into a noun (-tion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ing), usually with "of": the actor and the action vanish. Ask who did what, and suggest the verb as a query. Keep a nominalisation that points back to an action just described; that is a fair use (ch. 2, ch. 5).
- **Light verbs.** A weak verb hosting a zombie noun: make a decision, carry out an analysis, conduct a review, provide assistance, give consideration to. Query the plain verb (ch. 4).
- **Metaconcepts.** Abstract packing words (approach, framework, level, perspective, context, issue, model, process, role, strategy, tendency, variable). Two or more in one sentence is the place to look. Test: could you point at one in the street? (ch. 2)
- **Scare quotes.** Quotation marks around an ordinary idiom read as distance or doubt, and can flip the meaning. Fair uses: a real quotation, a word mentioned as a word, or a usage the writer disowns. Query the rest (ch. 2).
- **Hedges where a condition belongs.** Fluff qualifiers (somewhat, relatively, fairly, to some extent, seemingly, rather, sort of) blur a claim without saying when it fails. Ask the author to state the condition instead. Hedging is right where readers are adversarial (legal text), where a hypothesis must be stated exactly once, or where a tendency might be read as a law. Flag clusters, not one word (ch. 2).
- **Intensifiers as hedges.** In reference and technical text, very, highly and extremely can weaken a yes-or-no property. Leave them alone in blogs, letters and fiction (ch. 2).
- **Unexplained abbreviations.** An abbreviation used before its expansion, or never expanded, costs the reader more than it saves the writer. Ask for a short gloss on first use, or the full term when it appears only a few times. Table column codes that force a flip back are the same fault (ch. 3).
- **Role names.** Things named by their job in the writer's work ("the assessment item", "test conditions", "the control group") instead of what they are. Ask what the thing physically is (ch. 3, functional fixity).
- **Jargon a plain word replaces.** If a term costs nothing to swap for a common word, query it; if it must stay, ask for a few words of gloss on first use (ch. 3).

## How to raise them
- Name the reader first. A specialist reader may know the term or the abbreviation; the check is whether this reader does (ch. 3).
- When unsure what the reader knows, assume less, since writers overestimate (ch. 3).
- Group a repeated fault as one finding with a sample and a count ([line-copy-proof.md](line-copy-proof.md), Repeated fixes).
- Give the ground: reader harm, house rule or taste (Cameron, in [line-copy-proof.md](line-copy-proof.md)). Passives and nominalisations are choices, not errors; flag only where the actor is missing and matters (ch. 2, ch. 4).
- `EW stats` prints a `clarity` line: nominalisations per 1000 words, paragraphs dense with them, and light verb + noun pairs. Places to read, not findings. Legal and expository prose runs higher than notes and READMEs (about 28 against 11 per 1000 in the 2026-11-20 measurement), so compare within one piece, not against a norm. Abbreviations are not counted: the test list flagged thousands of familiar technical ones.
